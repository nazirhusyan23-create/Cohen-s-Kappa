Why not just always use percent agreement?

Percent agreement is the easiest number to compute and explain, but it can badly overstate reliability. If 90% of your items belong to one category, two raters could hit 90% agreement by both defaulting to that category most of the time — without actually agreeing on the harder, less common cases. Cohen's Kappa and Fleiss' Kappa both subtract out that chance component, which is why journals in psychology, medicine, and content analysis typically expect a kappa statistic rather than percent agreement alone.

Why not just always use kappa?

Kappa has its own well-documented quirk, sometimes called the kappa paradox: when one category is very rare, kappa can come out low even though raters agree almost all the time on the cases that matter. This is exactly why reporting both percent agreement and kappa side by side — which the Percent Agreement Calculator does automatically — gives a fuller picture than either number alone.

What if I have more than one rating type per item?

All three tools here assume categorical (nominal) ratings — labels like "present/absent" or "low/medium/high," not continuous scores. If your raters gave numeric scores on a continuous or ordinal scale (like a 1–10 severity rating), an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) or a weighted kappa is usually the more appropriate statistic, since it accounts for how far apart two ratings are, not just whether they matched exactly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I use Cohen's Kappa or Fleiss' Kappa?

Use Cohen's Kappa for exactly two raters who each rated every item. Use Fleiss' Kappa for three or more raters, especially if different subsets of raters rated different items.

Should I report percent agreement or kappa?

Most methodologists recommend reporting both — percent agreement for intuition, kappa for the chance-corrected number reviewers actually expect.

What if my raters gave numeric or ordinal scores instead of categories?

Kappa statistics are built for categorical ratings. For continuous or ordinal scores, an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) or a weighted kappa is usually more appropriate.
