What Is Fleiss' Kappa?

Fleiss' Kappa (κ) extends the idea behind Cohen's Kappa — chance-corrected agreement between raters — to situations with three or more raters. It was introduced by Joseph Fleiss in 1971 and is the standard statistic when a panel of coders, judges, or reviewers classifies the same set of items and you need one number summarizing how much they agree, beyond what chance alone would produce. Unlike Cohen's Kappa, Fleiss' Kappa doesn't require the same specific raters to rate every item — only that each item gets the same total number of ratings, which makes it well suited to rotating panels or crowd-rated datasets.

The Formula

For N items rated by n raters each into k categories, Fleiss' Kappa is calculated from the mean observed agreement across items and the expected agreement based on how often each category is used overall:

κ = (P̄ − Pₑ) / (1 − Pₑ) Pᵢ (per item) = [Σⱼ nᵢⱼ² − n] / [n(n − 1)] P̄ = mean of Pᵢ across all N items pⱼ = (Σᵢ nᵢⱼ) / (N × n) — proportion of all ratings in category j Pₑ = Σⱼ pⱼ²

As with Cohen's Kappa, a value of 1 means perfect agreement, 0 means agreement no better than chance, and negative values mean the raters agreed less than chance would predict.

Common Use Cases

Fleiss' Kappa shows up wherever a panel — not just a pair — classifies the same items: three or more clinicians independently grading the same medical images, a team of annotators labeling training data for a machine-learning model, multiple content moderators flagging the same posts, or a panel of judges scoring entries against a rubric. Reporting Fleiss' Kappa alongside your methodology gives readers a single, chance-corrected measure of how consistent your panel really was.

Worked Example

Suppose 4 content moderators each review the same 5 posts and mark each as Keep, Review, or Remove. For Post 1, all 4 moderators agree on Keep — a perfect match. For Post 3, 2 moderators say Review and 2 say Remove — a clear split. Once every post's rater counts per category are tallied (each row summing to 4, the number of raters), the calculator computes the per-item agreement Pᵢ for every post, averages them into P̄, subtracts the expected agreement Pₑ (based on how often each category was used overall), and divides by 1 − Pₑ to get the overall Fleiss' Kappa. A dataset like this one typically lands somewhere in the "Moderate" to "Substantial" range — try Load example data above to see the exact numbers.

Common Mistakes

The most frequent error is letting the number of raters per item vary — Fleiss' Kappa assumes every item received the same total number of ratings, and the calculator's warning banner flags rows that don't match. Other common issues: using Fleiss' Kappa when there are actually only two fixed raters (use Cohen's Kappa instead, since it also reports a confidence interval); forgetting that a low kappa can still mean high raw agreement if one category dominates (the "kappa paradox" — check percent agreement alongside it); and quoting Fleiss' Kappa for ordinal or numeric ratings, where a weighted kappa or ICC is more appropriate since it accounts for how far apart two scores are, not just whether they matched exactly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Fleiss' Kappa and Cohen's Kappa?

Cohen's Kappa only works for exactly two raters. Fleiss' Kappa generalizes the same chance-corrected idea to any number of raters, and doesn't require the same individuals to rate every item — just the same total ratings per item.

Do the same raters need to rate every item?

No — Fleiss' Kappa only needs each item rated by the same number of raters overall, not the same specific people each time, which is useful for rotating or crowd-rated panels.

What sample size do I need for Fleiss' Kappa?

There's no strict minimum, but small samples or heavily skewed category use make the estimate unstable. Aim for at least 20–30 rated items as a reasonable starting point.
