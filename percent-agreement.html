What Is Percent Agreement?

Percent agreement — also called raw agreement or observed agreement — is the simplest way to measure how consistently two raters classify the same items: the proportion of items where both raters gave the same rating. It's easy to compute and easy to explain to a non-technical audience, which is why it's still widely reported alongside more rigorous statistics. The catch is that it doesn't correct for chance — if two raters both default to the most common category most of the time, they can rack up a high percent agreement without actually agreeing on the harder, less common cases.

Percent Agreement vs. Cohen's Kappa

The two numbers answer slightly different questions. Percent agreement tells you how often the raters matched, full stop. Cohen's Kappa tells you how much better than chance that matching was, given how often each category was actually used. If your data leans heavily toward one category, expect the gap between the two numbers to be large — that gap is itself informative, and worth reporting alongside both figures.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is percent agreement?

It's the proportion of items where two raters gave the same rating, with no adjustment for chance — the simplest reliability statistic, but one that can overstate reliability when one category dominates.

Why is percent agreement usually higher than Cohen's Kappa?

Because it doesn't subtract out agreement you'd expect from chance. The more unevenly your categories are used, the bigger the gap between the two numbers tends to be.

Is percent agreement good enough to report on its own?

Most methodologists recommend pairing it with a chance-corrected statistic like Cohen's Kappa or Fleiss' Kappa, rather than reporting percent agreement alone.
