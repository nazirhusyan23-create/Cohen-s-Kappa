What Is Cohen's Kappa?

Cohen's Kappa (κ) is a statistic that measures agreement between two raters or two coding methods classifying the same set of items into categories, while correcting for the level of agreement you'd expect by chance alone. It was introduced by Jacob Cohen in 1960 and remains the standard way to report inter-rater reliability in psychology, medicine, content analysis, and machine-learning annotation work. Unlike simple percent agreement, kappa accounts for the fact that two raters can agree a fair amount purely by luck, especially when one category is used far more often than the others.

The Formula

Kappa is calculated from the observed agreement and the agreement expected by chance:

κ = (p₀ − pₑ) / (1 − pₑ) p₀ = observed agreement = (sum of matching diagonal cells) / (total items) pₑ = expected agreement = Σ (row total × column total) / (total items)²

A kappa of 1 means perfect agreement, a kappa of 0 means agreement equal to chance, and a negative kappa means the raters agreed less than chance would predict — a sign something is wrong with the category definitions or rater training.

Interpreting Your Kappa Score

The most widely cited interpretation scale comes from Landis & Koch (1977):

Kappa (κ)Strength of agreement
< 0.00Poor (worse than chance)
0.00 – 0.20Slight
0.21 – 0.40Fair
0.41 – 0.60Moderate
0.61 – 0.80Substantial
0.81 – 1.00Almost perfect

Some fields, particularly health research, prefer the stricter benchmarks proposed by Fleiss, which set 0.75 as the threshold for "excellent" agreement. Always report which scale you used alongside your kappa value.

Common Use Cases

Researchers reach for Cohen's Kappa whenever two independent judges classify the same items and reliability needs to be documented: two clinicians agreeing on a diagnostic category, two coders labeling the same set of survey responses or social media posts for a content analysis, two annotators tagging entities for a machine-learning training set, or two graders scoring the same essays against a rubric. In each case, kappa gives readers of your methods section a single, chance-corrected number to judge whether your coding scheme is trustworthy enough to build conclusions on.

Cohen's Kappa vs. Percent Agreement

Percent agreement is easy to compute but can be misleading: if 90% of items fall into one category, two raters could hit 90% agreement just by both defaulting to that category most of the time. Kappa subtracts out that chance component, so it is almost always lower than percent agreement — and the gap grows as category use becomes more unbalanced. Reporting percent agreement alongside kappa (both of which this calculator gives you) lets readers see both numbers and judge the gap for themselves.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good Cohen's Kappa value?

Under the Landis & Koch benchmarks, 0.61–0.80 is substantial agreement and 0.81–1.00 is almost perfect. Many journals treat 0.60 as a practical minimum for publication, but the right threshold depends on your field and how much is riding on the classification.

What's the difference between Cohen's Kappa and percent agreement?

Percent agreement just counts matches. Kappa subtracts out the agreement you'd expect by chance given how often each category is used, so it's a stricter, more honest number — and it's almost always lower than raw percent agreement.

Can Cohen's Kappa be negative?

Yes. A kappa near zero means the raters did no better than chance, and a negative kappa means they did worse than chance — often a sign of unclear category definitions or inconsistent rater training.

How many ratings do I need for a reliable kappa calculation?

There's no strict cutoff, but kappa gets unstable with very small samples or heavily skewed category use. As a rule of thumb, aim for at least 30–50 paired ratings before treating your estimate as stable, and always report the confidence interval.

Does this calculator work for more than two raters?

No — Cohen's Kappa is defined for exactly two raters. For three or more raters, you need Fleiss' Kappa, which generalizes the same chance-correction idea to any number of coders.
